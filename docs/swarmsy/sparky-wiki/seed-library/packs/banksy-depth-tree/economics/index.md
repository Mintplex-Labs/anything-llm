# Banksy Economics Layer

Status: expanded Wave 1 depth branch. Provenance labels used: cited claim, interpretation, disputed, needs-source.
Status: depth-tree foundation marker preserved for depth-tree guards.

This layer examines auction dynamics, cleanup economics, tourism, scarcity, value capture, regeneration, and fine-versus-publicity dynamics.

## Core Economic Contradiction

Banksy economics are not one clean story. The same public mark can look like vandalism to a property owner, a tourism asset to a city, a market object to a collector, a civic embarrassment to an authority, and a symbolic victory to an audience. That is the economic engine: value appears in different ledgers at the same time.

The local archive treats this as a contradiction, not a secret proof. A public wall may absorb security, cleanup, insurance, and maintenance cost. Media, tourism, collectors, auction houses, and the artist's myth system may capture attention value. Councils or museums may later stabilize that value by protecting, exhibiting, or narrating the work. Sparky should say who absorbs cost, who captures value, and which part is cited versus interpreted.

## Auction Spectacle

Claim `banksy-market-002` records that _Love is in the Bin_ sold at Sotheby's in 2021 for a reported GBP 18,582,000 including premium, using `banksy-love-bin-sothebys-2021` and `banksy-love-bin-guinness-2021`. This is a cited historic auction reference. Current records, current ownership, and new auction prices require live lookup.

Interpretation `banksy-market-003` says the shredded-painting event turned auction spectacle itself into part of the work's market narrative. That does not prove hidden coordination or market manipulation. It means the public event changed how the object was discussed, remembered, and valued. The auction house, press, social audiences, and collectors became part of the attention system around the work.

## Public Wall To Private Value

The `banksy-source-conflict-001` claim tracks reporting around _Slave Labour_, where removal from a public wall and proposed auction generated controversy. This is one of the clearest economic conflict cases in the local archive: the value of the work was partly created by public placement, public recognition, and local meaning, but the monetizable object could be extracted from that public context.

The source-conflict question is not only "who owns the wall?" It is also "who owns the social value created by public attention?" Street art can become valuable because people saw it in public, photographed it, argued over it, and made it part of place memory. Once removed, the physical object may still have market value, but the public-context meaning changes.

## Regeneration And Tourism Economics

Claims `banksy-regen-002` and `banksy-regen-003` anchor Dismaland as a 2015 project at the Tropicana site in Weston-super-Mare and public reporting that framed it as bringing wider attention to the town. This branch reads Dismaland as a regeneration economics case: a neglected or underused site becomes an attention magnet because the cultural signal is strong enough to redirect visitors, press, and public imagination.

The economic lesson is not "copy Banksy." The reusable pattern is: dead space + symbolic intervention + scarce time window + media hook + audience pilgrimage can produce temporary place value. The risk is that temporary attention can become a property, tourism, or branding story that benefits some groups more than others.

## Fine Versus Publicity Dynamics

Interpretation: in street-art economics, punishment can sometimes become publicity. That does not make illegal action advisable. It means enforcement, removal, or outrage may become part of the media loop. Sparky may analyze the mechanism, but must not give operational guidance for vandalism, trespass, illegal placement, evasion, or property damage.

## Modern Equivalents

- Influencer controversy loops where criticism increases reach.
- Limited drops where scarcity produces resale value.
- Meme accounts that turn audience participation into brand equity.
- AI-generated identity brands where the persona, not the product, becomes the tradable asset.
- Urban regeneration campaigns where "edge" and "authenticity" are packaged into place branding.
